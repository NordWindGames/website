Since the [first post](/blog/welcome/) the same question has come back to me in a few different shapes: fine, endless waves and a tower, but what is the story? It is a fair thing to ask, because so far I have only described the shape of [HoldStrong](/holdstrong/) and never what is actually going on inside it. So this one is nothing but story. There is almost no mechanics talk in it, and a handful of the things in it have not been written down anywhere public until now.

## The night the ice started walking

The Eternal Ice has always been up there. Every map of the far north ends at its edge, and for as long as anyone has bothered to keep count, the Vikings who walked into it did not walk back out. None of that is news; it is simply the shape the north has always had, a border you do not cross and do not think too hard about.

What is new is that the border started moving.

It began with an eclipse, on a night when every planet in the sky stood in a single line — an alignment that comes around once every five thousand years. Whatever happened up there, the ice has been advancing ever since, and it is no longer a place at the end of the map. It is a thing coming down towards everything that lives below it.

I want to be careful about what the ice actually is, because the temptation with a threat this size is to give it a throne and put a villain on it. The Eternal Ice has neither. There is no kingdom behind it, no council of frost lords, nobody who could be reasoned with or bargained down, and the creatures you fight are things it manifests rather than soldiers it commands. It wants nothing you would recognise as wanting. It simply comes closer.

That turns out to be useful well beyond the fiction, because it lets the difficulty curve carry story instead of just numbers. A later wave is not a slider being nudged upwards somewhere in a config file, it is the ice arriving. The light goes colder, the view gets shorter, and the ground you are standing on likes you rather less than it did an hour ago.

## What the tower is really made of

Every roguelite has to answer the same awkward question sooner or later. If you died, why do you still have the upgrades? Most games answer with a shrug and a currency, and I wanted an answer that was already sitting in the source material. The one I found is the part of this game I am proudest of.

Odin's favour does not grant you strength. It grants you permission to take something out of the hall.

Every morning the Einheri is sent back down, and he does not go empty-handed. An axe comes off the wall. A shield is carried out of the mead hall. Sometimes it is the bones of a warrior who fell beside him the day before and is finished needing them. Piece by piece, night after night, you are hauling Valhalla down to Rimehold and lashing it to the walls, and what stands there in the end is not really a building at all. It is a heap of grave goods that still shoots.

The practical side of that pleases me nearly as much as the fiction does. Rimehold is an outpost rather than a fortress, old and improvised and half swallowed by frost, with nobody left inside it who knows how to build anything. Valhalla, meanwhile, holds hundreds of thousands of the fallen, so the supply never runs dry no matter how long the progression runs.

I did not get there first, though. The earlier version had the tower growing out of Rimehold's own dead, the defenders who fell here being worked into the walls they died on, and in a single sentence that sounds better than what I ended up with. It only survives one sentence. There is nobody at the outpost who could mount them, their number does not grow alongside an upgrade tree the way an endless one needs it to, and worst of all they were already spoken for by something else in the fiction — which is the next thing I want to tell you about, so hold that thought.

## The god who never shows up

Three gods stand with you when the game launches: Thor, Loki and Týr. Each of them has an orb, and each helps in a way that is unmistakably his own. Thor arrives loudest and most visibly. Loki sends nobody at all and fakes someone instead. Týr kills nothing whatsoever — he pins a single enemy in place and does not let it leave, which is precisely the deed he gave his hand for.

Odin has none of this, and that is deliberate. He is not one of the powers you reach for in the middle of a fight; he is the authority standing behind the whole arrangement, the one who collects you when you fall and sends you back out when the sun comes up. Turning him into an ability would have made him smaller.

The trouble with deciding that is that it took him off the screen entirely, and a god you never see is a god the player quietly stops believing in. The way out came from the tower itself. A dead man's axe does not swing on its own, and something has to hold all that hauled-down Valhalla to the walls — a rune, scratched into each piece, binding it there. Odin hung nine nights on Yggdrasil to win the runes in the first place, so every rune glowing on that tower is him. He is in the picture constantly without ever needing a cooldown of his own.

Then the decision handed me something I had not planned for. If the runes are Odin's binding, damage to the tower is that binding coming apart, and a rune going dark stops being a health bar ticking down. It is one of the god's holds letting go.

## Two kinds of dead, kept apart on purpose

The Draugr that come at you out of the ice are Rimehold's own dead. They are the defenders who held this outpost before you did, dragged back up by the thing they died fighting, and they knew the tower they are now hammering on — which makes them the only enemy in the game with anything personal about them.

The tower, all the while, is carrying Valhalla's dead, the ones you brought down yourself.

It is the same raw material twice over, and I keep the two supplies strictly apart, which is also why the earlier version of the tower had to go. The ice takes the ones who fell here. You take the ones Odin has already claimed. Nobody gets counted twice.

Set the two side by side and the whole game says one thing in three different voices. **No one is allowed to stay dead.** The ice turns its dead into Draugr, Odin turns his into Einherjar, and the tower is what is left over when both of them are done.

## The moment I took out of the intro

You are never told that you are dead. You find it out by dying.

The opening minutes explain nothing at all. There is a defender, there is a tower, there is ice coming in, and that is the whole briefing. Then the last rune goes dark, and instead of the failure screen every instinct you have is bracing for, you are standing in a warm hall full of men who greet you like someone they have known for years. Everything reassembles itself in that second, backwards: this was not my first day here, and it will not be my last.

For a while that revelation lived in the intro, where it was safe and where I could be certain nobody missed it, and that is exactly what was wrong with it. Placed there, the best moment in the game becomes a paragraph of text the player clicks away to get to the part where something happens. So it moved onto the first tower death instead, and it costs me things I have to honour from here on: nothing before that moment is allowed to look like Valhalla. No warm firelight, no hall imagery, no mention of the Einherjar in any tutorial line anywhere.

And then I go and undercut it myself, with both eyes open. The store page sells the Valhalla loop openly, so a good share of players will walk in already knowing the turn. I sat with that trade for a while and took it anyway, because the loop is the reason anybody buys the game at all, and guarding a surprise that works exactly once at the price of being unable to describe my own game is the wrong way round. The moment still gets staged as a revelation. For most people it will simply land as a promise kept rather than a shock.

There is one more thing the game never tells you, and that one is permanent: who you are. No name, no face, no voice in your ear, and no place in the key art, which belongs to the tower and the ice. You are the replaceable one here. The ice is not.

## One thing I have not decided

Let me finish on something that is still open, because I promised in the first post to ask while the answers can still change anything.

The glowing rune belongs to Odin, and that much is settled. What is not settled is whether anyone else may carry one, and the reason it keeps surfacing is Týr, since a bound mark is about the most natural icon imaginable for the god who binds. The instant a second god wears runes, though, the rune stops quietly meaning Odin, and the very thing that put him back on the screen goes soft.

It has been sitting open since 10 September.
